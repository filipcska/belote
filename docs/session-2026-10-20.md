I'd put it at casual club level. It's a set of hand-written rules: no search, no simulation, and no reasoning about who holds which cards. ## What it does well

**Card memory (`CardTracker`):**
- It remembers every card played.
- When a player fails to follow suit, it notes that they have none of that suit.
- It notes the suit each player led most recently.
- From that it works out which cards can no longer be beaten in their suit.

**Leading a trick (when it plays first), in priority order:**
1. Play any card that can't be beaten, highest-scoring first.
2. Play back the suit its partner last led.
3. If it holds the contract in a suit game and has 3 or more trumps, lead its highest trump to draw out the opponents' trumps.
4. Otherwise, lead its lowest card from a suit no opponent is known to be out of.

**Following:**
- If the partner is winning and is last to play, or holds an unbeatable card, it throws on points. Otherwise it plays its cheapest card.
- If it can win the trick, it wins with the weakest card that does the job.
- If it can't win, it throws its least valuable card and keeps the partner's suit.

**Bidding:** it scores its first 5 cards against each contract (Jack and 9 count most in trumps, Aces and 10s in No Trumps). It bids the contract with the highest score over a fixed threshold. It doubles or redoubles when that score is at least 10 over the threshold.

## Where it's weak

**Mistakes that will show up in play:**
- **It can throw away the Jack of trumps.** When it has none of the led suit and its partner is winning, the rules let it play any card. It then picks the card worth the most points, and the Jack of trumps, at 20, beats everything. So it can dump the top trump onto a trick the partner had already won. See `chooseFollow` → `highestValue`.
- **It decides a card is unbeatable by looking at its suit alone.** A defender with no cards in that suit can still trump it. So it will lead off-suit Aces into opponents who are known to be out of the suit, and will pile points onto a partner whose winning card is about to be trumped.
- **It always takes a trick when it can.** The check on the trick's points and whether it's last to play has the same result on both branches (`BotPlayer.java`, the `atStake >= 10` block). So it never plays low to keep a strong card for later, and will spend a high trump on a trick worth nothing.
- **"Partner's suit" never expires.** It's the last suit the partner led at any point in the hand, even if that suit has since run out or been trumped.

**Things it doesn't consider at all:**
- In bidding:
  - what its partner or the opponents have bid
  - the match score
  - Belote (King + Queen of trumps) and other declarations when judging its hand
- The contract scores sit on different scales, so comparing them across contract types skews which one it picks.
- In play:
  - the 10 points for the last trick
  - taking every trick (valat)
  - whether the contract has already been made or lost
  - what the declarations revealed about other players' cards
  - which cards each player is likely to still hold
  - signalling to its partner
- In No Trumps and All Trumps, the "safe lead" filter switches off, so it just leads its lowest card.

## How to make it stronger
1. **Quick fixes:**
   - Never throw away trumps, or cards that can't be beaten, onto a partner's trick.
   - Count voids and outstanding trumps before treating a card as unbeatable.
   - Make the dead `atStake` check actually work, so it can play low instead of winning.
2. **Simulation:** for each legal card, play out the rest of the hand many times with random deals of the unseen cards that are consistent with the known voids and declarations, and pick the card that scores best on average. With only 32 cards this is cheap to run, and it's how strong card-game bots usually play. It would be the single biggest gain.
3. **Bidding:** take into account the partner's and opponents' bids, the declarations in hand, and the match score.

## New rule: leading when the partner called the contract

When its partner called the contract and the bot leads, it plays the top card of a suit that has exactly two unseen cards above it. That's the case in your example: the bot holds the A♠ and the J♠ and 9♠ are still out. The idea is that the caller probably holds the top card, so the next opponent is pushed into covering with the card in between, and the caller takes both.

- **Suit contract:** only the trump suit counts. Since players must beat a trump lead when they can, an opponent holding the 9♠ has to play it on the Ace.
- **All Trumps:** any suit counts, and the same must-beat rule applies (e.g. lead A♣ to pull the 9♣).
- **No Trumps:** any suit counts (e.g. lead K♥ when A♥ and 10♥ are out). Nobody is forced to beat the card here, so it's a weaker version. It also risks giving away the K if an opponent holds the Ace. Tell me if you'd rather turn it off for No Trumps.
- **Exceptions where it doesn't fire:**
  - the partner has shown they have none of that suit
  - both opponents have shown they have none of it
  - the bot's card is already unbeatable (it just plays it as a normal winner)
  - only one card is above the bot's (nothing for the opponents to cover with)

## Quick fixes

- **Keeps its strong cards:** it no longer throws trumps or unbeatable cards onto a trick its partner is already winning. When it discards, it throws away other cards first.
- **Watches for opponents trumping in:** a winning non-trump card is only played if no opponent who is out of that suit could still trump it. It also won't play it when fewer than two cards of the suit remain, since someone else must be out of the suit.

## What's in the way today

1. **The human seat is hard-coded.** `Seat.isHuman()` returns `this == SOUTH`. The engine (`GameEngine.java:77`), the bots (`BotPlayer.java:458`) and the UI all depend on it.
2. **The screen code drives the bots.** `BeloteApp` waits a moment and then calls `bot.chooseBid` / `chooseCard` (`BeloteApp.java:387-397`). In a networked game the host has to run the bots, not each player's window.
3. **The UI reads the engine directly.** It calls `engine.player(seat).hand()` and so on, so it can see every hand. A remote player must only receive what their seat is allowed to see.
4. **The table is drawn from South's point of view.** Each player should see themselves at the bottom.

## Suggested design: one host, everyone else connects to it

- **The host** is one copy of the game that clicks "Host game". It runs the only `GameEngine` and the bots, and it decides what is legal.
- **Clients** only send requests ("I bid 2♠", "I play the A♥"). The host checks them with the engine and sends each player an updated view of the game.
- **Who controls each seat.** Replace `isHuman()` with a per-seat controller: local human, bot, or remote player. Empty seats are filled by bots, so 2v2, 1+bot vs 2 humans and so on all work.
- **Each player gets their own view.** It shows their own hand, how many cards everyone else holds, the current trick, bids, scores and their legal moves. Nobody else's cards are ever sent, which also stops cheating by reading network traffic. The state is small, so sending a full view after every change is simpler and safer than sending only the differences.
- **Network messages.** Plain TCP connections with one JSON message per line; Java's built-in sockets are enough. JSON needs either Jackson or simple hand-written encoding, and I'd avoid Java's built-in serialization. The messages:
  - From client to host: `Join(name)`, `Bid`, `PlayCard`, `Ready`.
  - From host to client: `SeatAssigned`, `View`, `Event` (so cards can animate), `Error`.
- **Finding a game.** Version 1: type the host's IP address and port. Later: automatic discovery on the local network (UDP broadcast or mDNS), so games just appear in a list. On macOS the firewall will ask permission the first time someone hosts.
- **Lobby.** Players pick seats and teams, the host starts the game, and bots fill the gaps.
- **Disconnects.** A bot takes over the seat, and the player can come back using a token they got when they joined.
- **Threading.** The engine runs on one host thread, and network messages are queued onto it. On clients, updates are handed to the JavaFX UI thread with `Platform.runLater`.

## 1. Use a ready-made card set (best value)

- **Where to look:**
  - Several well-known open-source and public-domain sets exist. One example is the SVG-cards project, an open-source set of vector playing cards. I'm naming it from memory and can't check from here that it's still available, so verify it yourself.

- corner indices (rank and suit at the top left and bottom right, the latter rotated),

- a rounded border, a soft shadow and a patterned card back.
